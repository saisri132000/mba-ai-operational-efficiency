Abstract

This Operations Management project examines AI adoption and developers’ reported experiences using public Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 microdata. The archive contains 49,191 rows, while the publisher reports 49,009 responses in its published analysis. The reason for the 182-row difference has not been established; the present analysis documents its own cohort and does not claim exact reproduction of the published figures. The scope is employed professional developers: 28,774 records before question-specific and covariate exclusions. Five exploratory binary logistic regressions examine adoption, agent-productivity agreement, debugging frustration, complex-task ratings and trust. Models adjust for grouped work experience, organisation size and grouped country; experience is the exposure in the adoption model. Models of user experiences additionally compare usage frequencies. Robust HC1 standard errors and 95% confidence intervals are reported, with Holm correction for five nominated primary contrasts.

AI adoption model: n=19,773; 20+ versus under 5 years of experience; adjusted odds ratio 0.59, 95% CI 0.53–0.67.

Perceived agent productivity model: n=5,383; Daily versus monthly/infrequent agent use; adjusted odds ratio 8.00, 95% CI 6.77–9.45.

Results describe associations within selected respondents. Voluntary recruitment, missingness, reverse causation and self-reported outcomes prevent causal, national-population or objective financial claims. Operational recommendations concern controlled pilots, human review and measurement of real task time and quality.

Keywords: AI adoption; perceived productivity; developer workflow; trust; logistic regression; Operations Management; Stack Overflow.

1 Introduction and research scope

Software development is an operational process involving task allocation, cycle time, rework and quality control. AI may support this process while generating verification and debugging work. This study examines adoption and perceived benefits and frictions among employed professional developers, retaining Operations Management as the primary domain and statistical Data Analytics as the methodology.

The scope replaces the earlier OECD sector comparison for this revised report. It does not describe Chennai managers, all Indian employees or employer-level performance. OECD public aggregate analysis remains a separate earlier output. No datasets are pooled and no employer-worker linkage is inferred.

2 Evidence and conceptual framework

Stack Overflow’s methodology describes fieldwork from 29 May to 23 June 2025 and recruitment mainly through its own channels. Highly engaged users were more likely to encounter invitations. Its AI results page publishes question wording and descriptive findings on usage, trust, frustrations and agents. These publisher materials establish the measurement context, rather than validating causal effects.

The organising framework relates respondent work context and experience to adoption, then examines usage frequency alongside perceived benefits and frictions among users. Country and employer size are included as contextual adjustments. No causal pathway or mediation mechanism is identified. A favourable experience may encourage frequent use, frequent use may change perceptions, or both may reflect other factors.

Productivity agreement concerns perceived benefit. Debugging frustration concerns a selected problem, not measured rework minutes. Complex-task ratings concern perceived capability, not a task benchmark. Trust concerns confidence in AI output, not verified accuracy. These constructs are kept separate and are not combined into an operational-efficiency score.

The literature component in this revision is limited to the publisher’s evidence and statistical documentation. A broader peer-reviewed theoretical review and the institution’s preferred citation format remain for supervisor review. No claim of a complete systematic literature review is made.

3 Methodology and measurement

3.1 Source population and unit of analysis

Design: quantitative cross-sectional secondary analysis of publicly released respondent records. The unit is one survey respondent. Inclusion requires MainBranch = I am a developer by profession and Employment = Employed. Freelancers, students, hobbyists and other employment categories are outside the primary cohort. No probability weights are supplied or invented. Country means residence, not employer headquarters. Multiple respondents may share an employer; employer identifiers are unavailable.

M2 reconstructs the productivity item from the five AIAgentImpact agreement columns using exact semicolon-delimited labels. Exactly one rating is required. This avoids treating a blank agreement cell as disagreement. M3 excludes a response selecting both no problems and another option; a missing question is never recoded zero. M4 excludes the combined no use for complex tasks / do not know option. Missing outcomes are excluded model by model. Binary coding simplifies original ordered responses and loses information; zero does not mean a measured absence of benefit. Planning, neutrality and nonresponse remain distinct in source counts.

3.3 Predictors missingness and estimation

M1 compares work-experience categories: under 5 years (reference), 5–9, 10–19 and 20 or more. All models adjust for employer size: under 100 employees (reference), 100–999 and 1,000 or more. Country categories are United States (reference), Germany, India, United Kingdom, France and all other nonmissing countries pooled. These are the five most numerous countries in the full archive, not the OECD seven-country frame. Missing country is excluded rather than pooled. Organisation size I do not know, freelancer-only size, missing size and unavailable experience are excluded from complete-case models. Recorded experience outside 1–60 years is excluded as a covariate under a declared plausibility rule; zero cannot be inferred from blanks because the questionnaire instructed zero-experience respondents to leave it blank.

M2 uses agent frequency; M3–M5 use general AI-tool frequency. Each is categorical with monthly/infrequent as reference and weekly and daily contrasts. Restriction to current users is based on the relevant explicit usage question. No comparison of current agent users with nonusers can identify an agent-adoption effect on a user-only outcome. The primary M2 eligibility follows the agent-use answer even where another question is inconsistent; no unrecorded routing is assumed.

For each model log[p/(1−p)] = intercept + experience indicators + organisation-size indicators + country indicators + frequency indicators where applicable. Logistic maximum likelihood estimates odds ratios exp(beta). HC1 sandwich standard errors produce Wald 95% confidence intervals and p-values under independent-record assumptions. They do not correct voluntary sampling, common-method bias or unobserved employer clustering. All analyses are exploratory and chosen after inspecting survey variables, not preregistered.

Primary contrasts are 20+ versus under 5 years for M1 and daily versus monthly/infrequent use for M2–M5. Holm adjustment covers these five contrasts only. Other coefficients and the sensitivity fit remain exploratory; the confidence intervals are pointwise, not multiplicity-adjusted. No stepwise significance-based selection is performed. Complete cases assume missingness can be ignored conditional on the model, which is unverified and may bias estimates.

It does not mean eight times the productivity, an eightfold probability or a causal improvement. Daily users may self-select because they already perceive benefits. All design matrices have full rank and coefficient estimates are finite. Each GLM fit agrees within 0.000001 with a separately fitted Logit likelihood implementation. No fitting warnings were accepted. Variance inflation factors are below 10 and minority outcomes exceed 10 per estimated parameter. Calibration-bin counts, observed proportions, fitted probabilities, Brier scores and McFadden pseudo-R² are saved with the results. These are in-sample diagnostics, not out-of-sample validation; adequate convergence is not evidence that omitted variables or selection bias are absent.

As a coding sensitivity check, M2 was refitted after excluding neutral productivity ratings. The daily versus monthly/infrequent OR was 12.59 (95% CI 9.55–16.62; n=4,505), compared with 8.00 in the primary model. Direction is stable but magnitude depends materially on treatment of neutral responses. This sensitivity addresses coding only, not causality or missing-data bias.

Quality checks found no duplicate ResponseId values and 0 conflicting productivity ratings among eligible agent users. M3 excluded 257 contradictory no-problem selections. 9 employed professional records reported experience above 60 years and were treated as unavailable covariates. No imputation, fabricated participants or reconstructed OECD records were used.

5 Discussion recommendations and limitations

The results connect frequent AI use with more favourable reported capability and trust, and frequent agent use with productivity agreement. These patterns can reflect learning, task suitability, selection and reverse causation. The experience contrast in adoption shows heterogeneity within the respondent cohort, not evidence that experienced employees resist innovation. The debugging association is specific to one reported complaint and should not be generalised to total operational cost or quality.

5.1 Managerial implications

Managers can use the survey patterns to formulate local pilot questions. Begin with bounded tasks, define human-review responsibility and record actual completion time, rework, defects and acceptance quality before and during a pilot. Compare similar tasks and account for task complexity. Provide training in verification, prompt use and handling sensitive information. Assess who benefits and who incurs additional review work. These are recommendations for future evaluation, not interventions tested by this cross-sectional survey.

5.2 Limitations

Voluntary developer recruitment prevents representative inference to all developers, India, Chennai managers or firms. Country pooling masks differences and no employer-level clusters can be identified.

Cross-sectional self-reports do not determine temporal direction. Frequency and positive perceptions may influence each other. Task mix, skill, employer policy and other unmeasured factors can confound associations.

Question-specific nonresponse and complete-case exclusion can select respondents. Missing experience may include zero-experience respondents because of questionnaire instructions. No imputation resolves this ambiguity.

Dichotomising ordered ratings changes interpretation. The sensitivity model illustrates dependence on neutral-response handling. Robust errors do not repair sample-selection bias.

The public-archive count differs from the published count by 182 records. The reason remains unverified. No exact replication claim is made.

The analysis does not measure objective output, monetary savings, causal effects, verified code quality or managerial decision effectiveness. Model diagnostics are internal; no predictive deployment or external validation is claimed.

The revised research scope, academic literature coverage, format and unsigned front matter still require supervisor review.

5.3 Conclusion

The public survey supports a reproducible exploratory study of AI adoption and developer perceptions using genuine respondent records. Five fitted models answer five revised questions. Associations are suitable for discussion of workflow adoption, verification and perceived benefit, with explicit limits on generalisation and causality. OECD approval is not required for this independent dataset; acceptance of the revised dissertation scope remains an academic decision.
